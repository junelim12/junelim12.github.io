01 · The moment

One intro, done by hand

In June, the CEO of a Bay Area edtech startup asked me for one intro. They wanted to sell an AI English program into an SK affiliate and couldn't find the person who owned it.

Through my SK network I found the owner. Another AI English vendor was already working with the team, but they agreed to hear a proposal. The startup joined the pilot late, was invited to the RFP, and in October was selected as preferred bidder.

Nothing about the product changed. Only the information did.

02 · Defining the problem

Korea's giants want startups. Startups can't find the door.

Korean conglomerates want Bay Area innovation, and Bay startups want Korean enterprise revenue. From the outside, though, the need is hidden, the budget owner is unknown, and the only way in is a chain of intros, overseas sourcing lists and favors.

Timing makes it worse. Most buyers have already picked their shortlist before a vendor ever reaches them, so a startup that isn't known from day one never gets a shot.

04 · The solution

Post a need. Meet the owner.

The enterprise posts an RFP or a one-line brief. An AI layer pulls out the real need, budget, timeline and decision owner, scores it against a curated startup database, and sends invites to the best-fit startups. The enterprise gets a ranked shortlist, and the startups get a warm invite instead of a cold email.

A hypothetical example. It is the same deal from chapter 01, except the startup wouldn't have needed to find me.

06 · Why we win

Favors don't scale. Data compounds.

Today these deals run on favors. Overseas lists and accelerators see startups but not the real need. Brokers know one person. In-house innovation teams see their own need but keep partners to themselves. Kaybridge is built to see both sides.
